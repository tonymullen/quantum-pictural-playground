Chapter 3

Processes as diagrams

Follows PQP Chapter 3, §3.1–3.5 (printed pages 33–83) . The starred advanced material in §3.6 is not covered.

PQP starts with the language itself: processes drawn as boxes, systems drawn as wires, and equations between pictures. In this chapter you will learn to build diagrams in the Playground, read them as formulas, and check equations between them. You will also meet Dirac notation, which PQP presents as a one-line fragment of the diagrams.

Boxes, wires and process theories (PQP §3.1.1–3.1.2)

PQP calls anything with zero or more inputs and zero or more outputs a process, and draws it as a box. Its input wires come in at the bottom and its output wires leave at the top (§3.1.1). The wires carry labels called system-types, such as “real numbers”, “lists” or “bacon”, and an output may be wired to an input only if their types match. PQP’s examples range from functions and computer programs to cooking breakfast. The point is that the language of diagrams is about how processes compose, whatever the processes are.

Which processes there are, and what wiring them together means, is fixed by a process theory (Definition 3.1). A process theory gives the system-types, the processes between them, and a way of interpreting any diagram of those processes as a single process. PQP’s list of examples (Example 3.2) includes functions, relations, linear maps, classical processes and quantum processes.

The Playground’s process theory

The Playground interprets diagrams in one process theory: linear maps on qubits. Every wire has the same type, a qubit, and a box with $m$ inputs and $n$ outputs is a linear map from $m$ qubits to $n$ qubits. PQP defines linear maps in Chapter 5. In this chapter, what matters is that every diagram means something definite, so the Playground can compute with it and check equations.

A box whose label is a symbol, like f, stands for an arbitrary linear map of the right shape. When the checker meets one, it tries random choices; Exercise 3.10 looks at what that does and does not show. A state or effect labelled 0, 1, + or − is a particular basis vector, and a number node whose label reads as a number (2, 1/2, 0, 1/√2, −i) is that number.

Equations between diagrams (PQP §3.1.3–3.1.5)

A diagram is fully described by two things: which boxes it contains, and how they are connected. Where the boxes sit on the page plays no part. PQP states this as Corollary 3.5: diagrams that can be deformed into each other, without changing any connections, are equal. In short, only connectivity matters. PQP backs up the pictures with diagram formulas, which name every wire (Definitions 3.8–3.9), to show that diagrams are mathematics as rigorous as formulas. Once that is established, PQP uses the pictures.

PQP distinguishes two kinds of equation (§3.1.4):

  • A diagram equation holds in every process theory, because the two pictures can be deformed into each other.
  • A process equation holds in a particular theory: the pictures differ, but in that theory they mean the same process. PQP’s example, equation (3.5), is the identity $2m - 2n = 2(m-n)$ drawn as two different diagrams.

An equation also says which inputs and outputs correspond: in PQP’s pictures, by their positions on the page. Flip the inputs on one side of (3.5) and you get the false equation (3.6), even though only wires were rearranged. Exercise 3.4 has you find this in the Playground.

New equations come from old ones by diagram substitution (§3.1.5). If a piece of a diagram matches one side of a known equation, you may cut it out and paste in the other side, connected in the same place: the first input of the new piece to the wire that went to the first input of the old one, and so on (Remark 3.16). In the Playground you do exactly this. Select the piece in a proof step and paste a replacement over it (Cmd/Ctrl+V), and the wires reconnect in order. Exercise 3.7 practises it.

Circuits: ⊗ and ∘ (PQP §3.2)

PQP builds diagrams from two operations (§3.2.1–3.2.2):

  • Parallel composition $f \otimes g$ puts $f$ and $g$ side by side: “$f$ takes place while $g$ takes place”. It is associative, (3.11), and the empty diagram is its unit, (3.12).
  • Sequential composition $g \circ f$ wires the outputs of $f$ into the inputs of $g$: “$g$ takes place after $f$”. It too is associative, (3.13), and a plain wire is its unit, (3.14).

Note the order in $g \circ f$: the process written on the right happens first.

A circuit is a diagram built from boxes, identities and swaps using only $\otimes$ and $\circ$ (Definition 3.19). Theorem 3.22 characterises circuits by a property instead: a diagram is a circuit exactly when it has no directed cycle, that is, when you cannot follow wires from a box’s outputs and arrive back at its inputs. The proof slices the diagram into layers, and the Playground’s ∘⊗ view draws exactly such a slicing. Each box goes in the layer after the deepest box that feeds it; the layers are composed with $\circ$, and pieces side by side within a layer with $\otimes$.

Why the picture helps: diagrams beat algebra (PQP §3.2.4)

Write circuits with $\otimes$ and $\circ$ alone, and you need extra equations to say when two formulas mean the same circuit. The most striking is the interchange law: \[ (g_1 \otimes g_2) \circ (f_1 \otimes f_2) \;=\; (g_1 \circ f_1) \otimes (g_2 \circ f_2) \qquad (3.16) \] As formulas these look different. As diagrams they are the same picture, so in the diagrammatic language (3.16) holds automatically. Compressing a two-dimensional picture onto one line forces brackets on us, and then equations to undo the damage.

A consequence PQP derives from (3.12), (3.14) and (3.16): an effect followed by a state, with nothing connecting them, is the same as the two side by side.

Remark 3.25 explains the name: these are the circuits of quantum computing, and the Playground’s Circuit Editor builds them. The demo workbook has one, an H gate followed by a CNOT, with its PQP diagram and ∘⊗ layers. Chapter 4 will add cups and caps, which give diagrams that are not circuits but are still very useful.

Functions and relations (PQP §3.3)

PQP’s first two concrete process theories both have sets as their types:

  • In functions (§3.3.2), a box is a function. Sequential composition is the usual composition of functions, (3.18), and parallel composition applies two functions to the two halves of a pair, (3.19).
  • In relations (§3.3.3), a box relates each input to any number of outputs, possibly none. A relation is a function with added non-determinism. Relations compose by “there is a path from $a$ to $c$”, (3.21), and side by side by (3.22).

Functions are a sub-theory of relations (§3.3.4). Their types are the same, but they have very different properties as process theories, and PQP says that the extra relations are what make relations behave more like linear maps.

On paper

The Playground computes only with linear maps, so work through PQP Exercises 3.29–3.31 by hand. They ask which function the swap is, what some composites of NOT and CNOT compute, and what a relation built from three given relations is, using PQP’s four-step recipe after (3.23).

One part of Exercise 3.30 can also be done in the Playground. On the basis states $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, the Circuit Editor’s X and CNOT gates act just like the functions NOT and CNOT. Build Exercise 3.30’s circuits there and read the results in the Dirac editor. Chapter 5 explains why this works (PQP §5.3.4).

States, effects and numbers (PQP §3.4.1–3.4.3)

Three kinds of process are special (§3.4.1):

  • A state has no inputs. Operationally it is a preparation procedure.
  • An effect has no outputs. Operationally it is a test that came out “yes”.
  • A number has neither inputs nor outputs.

Plug a state into an effect and a number appears. PQP’s generalised Born rule, (3.25), reads this number as the probability that the test succeeds on that state.

In PQP’s pictures, numbers can float anywhere in a diagram, and their “little dance”, (3.24), shows that multiplying them is commutative in every process theory. This is the Eckmann–Hilton argument of Remark 3.33.

Open “3.2 States, effects and numbers” ↗

Amplitudes, not yet probabilities

In linear maps, the Playground’s theory, the number from a state and an effect is a complex number, an amplitude, not a probability: $\langle +|0\rangle = 1/\sqrt 2$. PQP anticipates this in Remark 3.34. Probabilities come out directly once diagrams are doubled into quantum maps (PQP Chapter 6, from §6.1.1), and the Playground does that too.

Zero (§3.4.2). If a state makes an effect impossible, their number is the “impossible” number, 0. Anything composed with an impossible process is impossible, so a zero process absorbs everything around it, (3.27). Note that 0 is not the empty diagram: the empty diagram changes nothing, while 0 swallows everything.

Equal up to a number (§3.4.3). Often only the shape of a process matters, not its overall factor. PQP writes $f \approx g$ when $\lambda f = \mu g$ for some non-zero numbers $\lambda, \mu$ (Definition 3.39). This is the Playground’s $\approx$: the checker accepts $\approx$ when the two sides differ by a non-zero factor, and says what the factor is. Without the non-zero condition everything would be $\approx$ everything else, since $0 \cdot f = 0 = 0 \cdot g$.

Dirac notation (PQP §3.4.4)

Most quantum-information texts write processes in Dirac notation, introduced by Dirac, who also named its two halves the bra and the ket . PQP §3.4.4 presents it as what is left of a diagram when it has to fit on a single typewritten line. Cut the diagram up, turn the pieces sideways, and write them in a row. This section makes that dictionary explicit, and gives the standard meaning of each piece from a widely used textbook, Nielsen and Chuang .

Caveat: bras. In Nielsen and Chuang, $\langle\psi|$ is always the dual of the ket $|\psi\rangle$, its conjugate transpose (Fig. 2.1). In PQP Chapter 3, $\langle\pi|$ is just the name of an effect π, and nothing yet relates it to a state with the same name. PQP says it has departed slightly from the usual Dirac notation here (Remark 3.41), and supplies the missing link, turning a ket into a bra, in §4.3.3. The Playground follows PQP: an effect labelled π is a bra named π, while an effect labelled 0, 1, + or − is that basis bra.

In the Playground: Generate writes a diagram by these rules. The Dirac editor reads two kets side by side as their tensor product, $|0\rangle|+\rangle = |0+\rangle$, as Nielsen and Chuang do (§2.1.7); a ket followed by a bra, $|0\rangle\langle 1|$, is still the outer product.

Why the picture helps: where one line runs out

The rules work smoothly for states, effects and boxes stacked in a column. They break down for boxes side by side, with several wires in and out. Then you have to write $\otimes$ explicitly, or pad with identities, $f \otimes I$, or use subscripts that only make sense if you already know the system (PQP’s D10). Brackets and the extra equations of §3.2.4 come back with them. The diagram needs none of this, because it uses the second dimension of the page. Exercise 3.8 lets you feel the difference.
